First Breath Underwater: What to Expect From a Try Dive on Gili T

11 min read · Practical guide

Almost every certified diver remembers the first time they breathed underwater. It feels strange, a little unnatural and then, within a minute or two, quietly wonderful. A try dive is designed to give you that moment without a full course, a long commitment or any previous experience. This guide walks through the experience step by step, from the questions to ask before you book to the quiet satisfaction of surfacing, so that nerves are replaced by curiosity.

If you are considering Try Diving Gili Trawangan as a holiday activity, it helps to know what actually happens and what is expected of you. Programme details, depth limits and age rules are set by PADI and applied by each centre, so treat this as a friendly preview and confirm the specifics with the team who will look after you.

Why a Try Dive Is the Easiest Way to Start

A try dive, often called Discover Scuba Diving, is an introduction rather than a certification. You learn a handful of essential skills in shallow water, then dive with a PADI professional beside you the whole time. There is no exam, no long theory block and no pressure to commit to anything afterwards. You simply find out whether the underwater world suits you.

That low-pressure structure is its biggest strength. Many people arrive convinced they are not strong swimmers or that they will panic, and discover that the process is far gentler than they imagined. Your instructor controls the pace, explains each step and stops whenever you need a moment. Nobody expects you to be a natural, and nobody is watching to judge you.

It is also a practical way to try a new activity during a holiday. A morning is often enough to experience the sensation of weightlessness, see marine life up close and decide whether you want to learn more. Even if you never dive again, you leave with a memorable experience and a better understanding of why so many people fall in love with the sport.

Before You Book: Health, Age and Honest Questions

Safety begins before you reach the water. You will complete a medical questionnaire that asks about conditions affecting the lungs, heart, ears, sinuses and other areas. Answer it honestly. If you tick a box that needs further advice, a doctor may need to approve your participation, and it is better to find that out in advance than on the morning of the dive.

PADI sets minimum ages for introductory programmes, and younger participants may have different depth limits and extra supervision. If you are booking for a family, ask about these rules early so everyone knows what to expect. Pregnancy is not compatible with scuba diving, and recent illness, especially anything affecting your ears or chest, is worth mentioning to the team.

Questions worth asking

  • How many participants does each instructor look after?
  • What equipment is included and how is it fitted?
  • Where will the practice and the dive take place?
  • What should I bring and when should I arrive?

Good centres answer these questions clearly and without hurry. If something is vague, ask again. Feeling informed is part of feeling safe.

What to bring is usually simple: swimwear, a towel, sunscreen that is gentle on the reef, and a change of clothes. Leave jewellery and valuables at your accommodation, and wear something comfortable under your wetsuit. Having everything ready the night before removes small stresses and lets you arrive at the dive centre unhurried and ready to enjoy the experience.

The Morning Briefing: Meeting Your Instructor

Your experience starts with a conversation. The instructor introduces themselves, checks your paperwork and explains how the session will unfold. This is the moment to mention any worries, whether about breathing, depth, ears or simply being in open water. A good instructor will adjust the plan to suit you and will take your concerns seriously.

You will usually receive a short briefing covering the key rules of diving: breathe continuously, never hold your breath, move slowly and signal if you need help. These ideas are simple, and the instructor repeats them in different ways until they feel natural. If you do not understand something, say so. Repetition is a normal part of the process and nobody will mind.

It also helps to arrive rested and calm. Eat lightly, drink water, and avoid alcohol the night before. A relaxed mind learns faster than an anxious one, and sleep makes a surprising difference to how comfortable you feel once you are in the water. Think of the briefing as a conversation between people who both want the dive to go well.

Good to know. Tell the instructor if you wear glasses or contact lenses. They can suggest options so you can see clearly underwater.

Getting Comfortable With the Equipment

Scuba gear looks complicated at first glance, but each piece has a simple job. The mask lets you see, the fins help you move, the regulator delivers air from the cylinder, and the buoyancy control device helps you float or sink gently. Your instructor sets up the equipment, shows you how it works and checks that everything fits properly before you enter the water.

Take your time with the mask. A good fit should feel snug but not painful, and a quick test on land prevents leaks later. If water does get in, it is a normal and easily fixed situation that you will practise. Many first-timers worry about the mask more than anything else, yet it is often the skill they enjoy most once they realise how easy it is.

The weight of the gear can surprise people on land, but in the water it feels almost weightless. Walk carefully, accept help with the cylinder and let the instructor carry the heavy parts. Once you are floating, the equipment fades into the background and your attention shifts to breathing, movement and the new world around you.

Learning the Basic Skills in Shallow Water

Before you go deeper, you practise a few essential skills in a calm, shallow area. You learn to breathe through the regulator, recover it if it falls out of your mouth, clear a little water from your mask and equalise the pressure in your ears. Each skill is demonstrated first, then repeated until you feel comfortable.

Equalising is the one many people ask about. As you descend, pressure presses on your eardrums, and you relieve it by gently pinching your nose and blowing softly, or by swallowing and moving your jaw. Do it early and often, before you feel discomfort, and never force it. If something hurts, signal your instructor and ascend slightly. This is routine, not a sign that you are doing anything wrong.

You also learn simple hand signals for communicating underwater, such as okay, up, down and a problem. Because you cannot speak, these gestures become your language. Practising them in shallow water makes them feel natural, and it builds trust with your instructor. By the end of this stage, most people are surprised by how quickly the strangeness fades.

The Moment You Go Under: Breathing and Calming Down

The first few breaths are the memorable ones. You descend slowly, hold on to a line or your instructor’s hand if you wish, and breathe slowly and deeply. Most people instinctively breathe too fast at first. Focus on long, steady inhales and exhales, and the sensation of calm follows quite quickly.

It is entirely normal to feel a flicker of anxiety. Your body is doing something unfamiliar, so a little caution makes sense. If you feel uneasy, pause, look at your instructor and signal. They will stop, check on you and help you settle. A moment of stillness often turns nervousness into delight, because you realise you are completely supported.

After a minute or two, something shifts. You notice that you are floating, that the sound of your own breathing is steady and that the water around you is full of detail. This is the part people talk about afterwards. It is not about adrenaline or bravery, but about a calm, curious feeling that is hard to find anywhere else.

Exploring With Your Instructor: What You Might See

Once you are comfortable, you move gently along the reef with your instructor, who keeps control of direction, depth and pace. Your job is to breathe, relax and look around. Gili Trawangan is well known for its warm water and variety of marine life, and a shallow dive can still reveal colourful fish, coral formations and, if you are lucky, a sea turtle.

Wildlife varies from day to day, so avoid expecting a particular animal. Treat each dive as a quiet exploration and enjoy the small details, such as the way light moves across the sand or how fish gather around coral. Your instructor may point things out with signals, and you can respond with an okay sign or a gesture of surprise.

Water clarity and currents change with the season and the tides, so your instructor chooses a spot that suits the day’s conditions. Trust that decision. Experienced guides read the water constantly, and a calm, sheltered area is often the best place for a beginner. If you want photos, ask whether the centre offers a camera or a photographer, because carrying your own gear can distract you from the basics.

Respect the reef while you are there. Keep your hands to yourself, avoid touching marine life and stay clear of the coral. Beginners often worry about floating too close, but your instructor guides your position and shows you how to hover with control. Good habits begin on your very first dive, and they protect the environment you have come to enjoy.

After the Dive: Rest, Hydration and Flying Rules

Surfacing is gradual, and most people emerge grinning. Take a moment to share what you saw with your instructor and ask any questions that came up. Drink water, eat something and rest. Diving is more tiring than it appears, and many people feel pleasantly sleepy afterwards.

Be careful about flying. After scuba diving, your body needs time to release the extra nitrogen it absorbed, so planes and high altitudes should be avoided for a period. Guidelines usually recommend waiting at least 12 hours after a single dive and longer after repeated diving, but check with your instructor for the advice that applies to you. Plan your activities so that your dive is well before any flight.

Also avoid heavy alcohol straight afterwards, and keep an eye out for any unusual symptoms such as joint pain, dizziness or unusual tiredness. These are uncommon after a shallow introductory dive, but if you notice anything concerning, speak with the dive centre or a doctor promptly. Sensible habits keep the experience enjoyable from start to finish.

Take a little time to reflect on the experience as well. Talk with friends or family about what you noticed, what felt easy and what felt unexpected. Putting the dive into words helps you remember it, and it often reveals how much you learned in a short time. Those reflections make the next dive even more enjoyable.

Turning a Try Dive Into Your Next Step

For some people, one dive is plenty. For others, it is the first page of a long story. If you enjoyed the experience, ask your instructor how your try dive may count toward further training, since some programmes allow you to continue into a certification course without starting from scratch.

You might also consider another try dive on a different day to build confidence at your own pace. Repeat experiences help you relax and notice details you missed the first time. Whatever you choose, there is no deadline. Diving rewards patience, and a calm, enjoyable start is the best foundation for everything that follows.

If you decide to continue, think about what you want from diving. Do you picture relaxed holiday dives, travel to new places or a deeper skill set? Your answers shape the path, from the entry-level certification to specialty courses and beyond. Talking it through with your instructor helps you choose a step that feels exciting rather than overwhelming.

Finally, keep a record of your experience. Write down what you saw, how you felt and what surprised you. Those notes are a useful guide if you decide to continue, because they show what you enjoyed and what you would like to improve. Many divers say that their logbook becomes a treasured souvenir, and it begins with that very first entry.

Key Takeaways

  • A try dive is an introduction, not a certification, with an instructor beside you throughout.
  • Answer the medical questionnaire honestly and ask about age rules and group sizes before you book.
  • Breathe slowly, equalise early and signal whenever something feels wrong.
  • Practise skills in shallow water until they feel natural.
  • Respect the reef, keep your hands to yourself and enjoy the small details.
  • Wait before flying and rest properly after your dive.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to know how to swim?

You should be comfortable in the water, and the team may ask you to show basic swimming ability. You do not need to be an athlete, and buoyancy equipment supports you during the dive. Mention any concerns when you book.

How deep will I go?

Introductory dives stay shallow, with depth limits set by PADI that your instructor will explain. The shallow range is where much of the colour and life is, so you will not miss out.

Can children try scuba diving?

PADI allows children from a minimum age to join introductory programmes, with extra rules and supervision. Ask the centre which options apply to your child’s age and experience.

Will it hurt my ears?

It should not. Equalise early and gently as you descend, and signal your instructor if you feel pressure or pain. Most people manage easily with a little practice.

What if I feel nervous halfway through?

That is completely normal. Signal your instructor, pause and breathe slowly. You can end the dive at any point, and the team will support whatever decision you make.

Final Thoughts

If that first breath underwater has left you curious about doing it properly, the natural next step is certification, which is explored in the next article of this series: Learning to Dive on Gili T: A Step-by-Step Look at the PADI Open Water Course.

With careful briefing, patient guidance and respect for the sea, it can be one of the most memorable hours of a holiday. Whether it becomes a single story to tell or the start of a lifelong hobby, it is a gentle, safe way to discover a world that most people never see.
